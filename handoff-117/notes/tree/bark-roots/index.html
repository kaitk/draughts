The maple's bark, junctions and roots: research and CPU prototypes (round one)

Task 117, 2026-11-26, branch room-light. CPU only, in Python with numpy and Pillow; nothing under src/ changed. The scripts are in src/ next to this page: bark.py (the tile's growth), render.py and tile_figs.py (the tile on a tube, beside the photos), scene.py, junction.py and rast.py (the junctions and the flare on today's skeleton, with a small rasterizer), today.py (today's tile in numpy), photo_figs.py. The skeleton is today's (../branches/dump/tree-none-whole.bin); the branch skeleton is being chosen in parallel, so the junction pictures show the method, to be done again on the chosen skeleton.

Proposal in short. (1) Bark: grow the tile once at startup by the fracture of a stretched outer layer (after Lefebvre and Neyret 2002). Every crack keeps the time it opened, so one run gives every age: smooth twig bark, first thin cracks on branches, the interlaced ridges of a limb, and the deep dark furrows of the old trunk. Bake 4 age layers; the shader blends the two either side of a vertex's age. Strength comes from a true height-derived normal, a cavity (occlusion) term and the AO microshadow, not from moving the mesh. (2) Junctions: "swell and weld" on the tube mesh as it is: the child swells into a collar, the parent gets a bump round the child's foot and a raised fold in the crotch, the child's first rings are laid from the contact line with a fillet, and welded a few millimetres under the parent. The bark's grain bends round each foot by a flow field, and the two charts blend by height at the contact. (3) Flare and roots: lobes in the trunk's lowest rings over each root, and 4-7 flattened root tubes welded the same way, diving under the lawn. Cost on the high tier: about +35k triangles over today's 18k tube triangles; much less on the standard tier, almost nothing on the phone.

1. What the photos show

Two looks of old Norway maple bark appear in the photos: the park maples (dense, short, wavy ridges with very dark deep furrows, mossy) and the lawn tree (narrow raised ridges in long diamonds over broad furrows). The prototype sits between them; see open question 1.

2. Methods, with their sources and parameters

Bark

MethodWhat it does, its parametersFor us
Lefebvre and Neyret 2002, "Synthesizing Bark" (HAL) Circular 1D strips across the trunk, each a chain of stiff bark elements and soft fracture elements. Growth stretches the strip; a crack starts where the relative stretch passes a threshold S + A·rnd, at a weak point from a weakness map. It runs into the next strip when an Inglis-ratio test passes; its way is a·slope + b·rnd, where a depends on the distance to the nearest crack, so close cracks follow each other, interlace, and leave thin strips between them. A fracture-stiffness curve Kf(strain) sets how a crack opens; later growth widens it. 133 strips took 49 s on a 900 MHz Pentium III. No numbers for S and A are given. No code. Chosen, simplified to a tile (section 3). It is the only method that makes the age continuum come out of the process itself.
Federl and Prusinkiewicz 2004, finite-element fracture on growing surfaces (thesis) A growing core and a bark layer of 6-node wedge elements. A crack starts where the largest principal stress passes the material's threshold, across that stress; it grows while the released energy exceeds the energy to form it (Griffith). Adaptive time steps, mesh refined near cracks. No constants published, no code. The physics behind the simplification; far too slow for startup.
Kratt et al. 2015, Woodification (PDF) Cambial growth as a moving surface (a deformable simplicial complex) whose speed comes from the skeleton; cracks by the Iben and O'Brien stress field: stress spreads over the mesh, a crack edge is inserted where it passes a threshold, with extra stress at crack tips; crack width from the distance to the crack's end, depth follows width. Parameters: cracks per step, relaxation steps, tip stress 0..1, snap angle. 15-162 ms per growth step, 100-200 steps. Good ideas (width by distance to the crack's end; growth stretching the bark), but a full surface simulation. Not needed for a tile.
Desbenoit, Galin, Akkouche 2005, "Modeling cracks and fractures" (PDF) A crack pattern mapped onto a surface and carved as a swept volume; very thin cracks at any resolution. Generic, not bark. Not used.
Wang, Wang, Liu, Hu, Guo 2003, "Interactive modeling of tree bark" From one bark photo: 36 filter responses per pixel clustered into 25 textons, a height field set per texton, a few minutes of editing. Not a growth model. Not used: needs a photo with a licence, and gives one age.
Microshadow from AO: Brinck and Maximov, "The Technical Art of Uncharted 4", SIGGRAPH 2016; also Chan, "Material Advances in Call of Duty: WWII", SIGGRAPH 2018 course A cheap shadow from the occlusion map: saturate(|n·l| + 2·ao² − 1), so furrows go dark when the sun grazes the surface. (Formula as widely quoted; the talks themselves not re-read for this page.) Chosen for the standard tier.

3. The bark tile

How it grows

The tile is 256 × 512 texels for 25 × 50 cm, as today, wrapping both ways. Time t runs from 0 (a twig) to 1 (an old trunk). From T_CRACK = 0.42 to 1, in 36 steps:

  1. The widest plate the cork can hold shrinks as the stem grows: w = 20 mm · t−1.6, times a strength noise (±35 %). Any plate (a run between cracks across a row) wider than that must crack.
  2. Among the overstretched texels, the weakest of a few random ones opens (a weakness map: noise stretched along the stem, warped).
  3. The crack runs up and down the stem row by row. Its lean starts random (σ 0.22 texel per row), drifts, and decays; it steps to the weakest of three texels near where the lean takes it; a crack within 10 texels pulls it over (probability 0.8) and it stops when it meets one. That is Lefebvre and Neyret's a·slope + b·rnd with the pull by distance: cracks interlace and join, so ridges part and join into long diamonds. It runs at least 60-260 texels (3-10 spacings), then stops where the plate is no longer too wide.
  4. After t = 0.75, short cuts across old ridges, crack to crack (the thin strips between cracks break again): ridges become scales.

A snapshot at age t keeps only the cracks opened by then. Each furrow's half width grows with its own age (1.2 + 3.6 texels), its depth with its age and with the cork's thickness (up to 9 mm at t = 1). The ridge profile: steep shoulders into a narrow furrow, a rounded crown. Then a little macro relief, cork grain, and for young bark fine streaks and lenticels. The cavity is how far a texel sits under its neighbourhood at 2, 5 and 10 texels; the albedo is weathered grey on the crowns and dark brown in the furrows. The run takes 3 s in Python (278 cracks, 24k crack texels); a snapshot 0.24 s.

At the window's distance and close, lit from the side

The user asked (2026-11-26) for relief strong enough to show the stark contrast of old maple bark: deep dark furrows between raised ridges, as in the photos, not flat or washed out, and smoother on young wood. Here the strength comes from three maps, none from the mesh: a normal from the true height (slopes up to about 60°), a cavity term down to 0.1 in the deepest furrows (it darkens the sky's light), and the AO microshadow (it darkens the sun's light where it grazes). The furrows' albedo is also darker (0.05 against 0.20 on the crowns). The mips are plain box averages as the GPU's, so the far views show what mips do to the relief.

Into the game

  • Layers. 4 age layers (t = 0.45, 0.65, 0.82, 1), a 2D array texture, RGBA8 each: normal across, normal along, height, cavity. The albedo comes in the shader from the height (crown or furrow colour) and a slow blotch; lichen and moss would be a second, small map. 4 × 0.7 MB with mips = 2.8 MB (today 0.7 MB). The shader blends the two layers either side of the vertex's age; twigs (ribbons) need none.
  • The vertex's age. From its radius now; from the skeleton's own age (years from the tip, or level) once the skeleton is chosen, since "a thin old branch is not young".
  • Startup. Estimated, not measured: the growth about 5-10 ms in Goose if the plate widths are kept per row and updated only where a crack ran (the Python recomputes them whole); 4 snapshots with a two-pass chamfer distance and separable blurs about 15-25 ms. So about 25-35 ms once, inside the bark's share of the 60 ms "atlas and bark" today; to be measured.
  • Tiers. Standard: normal + cavity + microshadow. High: the same, plus a parallax shadow march (16-24 taps) only if a close view comes; at the window's distance it is not needed. No displacement of the mesh.

4. Junctions and the flare, on today's skeleton

The method: swell and weld

  1. Rings. Extra rings on the parent round each child's foot, more sides on the trunk; the child's first M = 10 rings are for its foot.
  2. Collar. The child's radius swells toward its foot: r (1 + c e−s/(λ r) (1 + u·below)), c = 0.22, λ = 1.2, u = 0.3 (more on the underside, after Shigo).
  3. Bumps on the parent. Out along the normal: the trunk collar 0.28 rc e−(d/1.1 rc)² by the distance d to the child's surface, and the branch bark ridge, a fold 0.10 rc high and 0.22 rc wide along the contact, only on the crotch side (where the two surfaces face each other). The child's chain is carried on back past its foot for this, so no point finds its round end.
  4. The foot. For each line along the child (one per side), find where its swollen surface meets the bumped parent; lay the M rings from there over 2.2 rc, closer near the contact, with a fillet that adds kf (1 − f/kf)² by the distance f to the parent (kf = 0.35 rc), fading out by the end of the foot. Each line is monotone, so rings never fold.
  5. Weld. Any child vertex still inside the parent is pushed out to 4 mm under its surface, so the parent's mesh covers the seam. No shared vertices, no new topology.
  6. Grain. The parent's round coordinate is bent round the foot by the stream function of a flow past a cylinder, ψ = X (1 − 0.8 / r²) over the foot's ellipse (rc across, rc/sin θ along). Near the contact (0.5 rc) each vertex also carries the other part's chart and a weight, 0.5 on the contact; the fragment samples both and blends them by height (the higher ridge wins).
  7. Flare. The trunk's lowest 1.5 m get lobes: per root 0.95 A R e−(Δφ/w)² e−h/H, 6 roots, strength A 0.25-0.6, uneven azimuths with gaps, flare height H 0.3-0.8 D. Each root is a tube of 14 sides flattened to 0.55, swelling 60 % toward the trunk, its centre a little over the ground then diving 2.4 radii under it past 0.5-1.25 of its reach (0.25-0.8 D); its foot laid and welded like a branch's. Its grain runs along it; its age 0.82-1.

Tried first and set aside: moving both meshes onto one implicit surface smin(dparent, dchild, k) − ridge, each vertex kept on its own side of the crease. With tubes of 12-48 sides the crease fell between vertices and came out jagged, and the polynomial smooth minimum's fillet (k/4 at most) was too small for a collar.

5. Fitting it into the tube mesh, and the cost

  • Flat data. Every step is per vertex over the arrays wood_mesh already fills: rings with a side count per tube, a frame carried along without twist. A junction needs only the parent's chain, the child's chain, its foot's arc length, and a few constants; the root lobes need 4-7 (azimuth, strength, width, height) records. Fixed tables: MAX_JUNCTIONS and MAX_ROOTS = 8, sized at startup. Nothing in the frame loop.
  • Vertex. One more float2 (the other chart) and a float (its weight); the age goes in a spare channel. The fragment samples the tile twice only where the weight is above 0, else once.
  • Light. crown_light is computed per ring, not per vertex, so extra rings cost about 800 grid lookups each; 800 extra rings is about 3-5 ms.
  • Growth time (estimated): the feet, 20 sides × 24 samples × 2 field calls per junction, about 100 junctions, about 3-6 ms; bumps on the parents about 1 ms. With the light, about +10 ms over today's 6.5 ms, inside the 100 ms.

6. What waits for the chosen skeleton

  • Where the junctions are, how many, and their radius ratios: a collar only where the child is clearly thinner (below about 0.7 of the parent); a codominant fork gets a ridge and perhaps a seam of included bark instead.
  • Stubs and broken leaders: a collar of woundwood round each stub (the same swell, closing over the cut).
  • Each segment's age, for the bark's layer, if the skeleton keeps one.
  • The trunk's lean and the hanging limb: the flare's lobes stronger on the side the tree leans to resist.
  • The final triangle counts and times: measure them in Goose on the Mac and the tablet.

7. Open questions for the user

  1. Which old bark? The park maples' (dense short wavy ridges, very dark furrows) or the lawn tree's (narrow raised ridges in long diamonds over broad furrows)? The tile sits between; both are a few knobs apart (plate width, furrow width, cuts across).
  2. Moss and lichen: the park photos are mossy low on the trunk. Add the mask now (a second small map, by height, shade and the ground), or later?
  3. Roots' exposure: a tidy lawn (short humps, as now) or more worn ground with longer roots showing?
  4. The bark's age: by radius (now) or by the skeleton's own age?
  5. The grain at a junction: keep the cheap two-chart blend (a smudged band a few centimetres wide), or carry one chart across each junction (solve a flow field on the foot's vertices at startup, more CPU, no band)?
  6. Worth it behind the pier? The base isn't seen from the library today. Build roots and flare now on all tiers, or the high tier only until a view shows them?
  7. Parallax: leave it out until a close view exists? At the window's distance it adds little (section 3).
